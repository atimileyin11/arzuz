What rent actually costs in Malete

Rent around KWASU is quoted per session, not per month. That one difference is where most students lose money, because a session is roughly nine months of use spread over twelve months of paying. When you compare an off-campus room in Malete with anything you have rented before, convert it to the same unit first — per session — and then compare what is inside the number.

This page is about how to read a rent, not about a price we promise. Arzuz does not publish an "average rent in Malete", because an average of a handful of rooms is not a price — it is a rumour with a number on it. Every listing carries the owner's own asking price, and that is the only figure worth acting on.

Four things hiding inside one number

Two rooms ₦40,000 apart can be the same price by the time you have lived in them for a session. Before you compare, find out where each of these sits:

  • Water. A borehole that runs when the grid is off is worth money. A tap that works only when there is light is a cost you pay in time and buckets.
  • Power. Is there a NEPA connection, a generator, solar, or nothing? And crucially: who buys the fuel, and how is a generator's running time decided? "Generator backup" means very little if it runs for two hours at night.
  • Bathroom. A private bathroom is the single most common reason one room costs more than the room next door. A shared one is not automatically worse — a shared one with two people is not a shared one with fourteen.
  • Security. A locked gate and a compound with someone living on it change what a room is worth, especially if you keep a laptop in it.

What pushes the price up

Four things account for most of the difference between two rooms in Malete:

  • Distance from the gate you actually use. A compound closest to the main gate costs more and fills first. Ten minutes further out usually buys more space and a lower rent — the trade is real, and it is yours to make.
  • The walk being measured, not guessed. "Five minutes from campus" is the most abused sentence in student housing. Arzuz measures it from a pin the owner drops at the gate, so two rooms on the same street can honestly show different walking times.
  • What genuinely works in the room. Utilities are ticked by the person listing the property, and checked when the property is inspected. Only what works is listed.
  • Whether the compound is managed. Somebody who answers a message about a broken tap is worth part of the rent.

What gets added on top

The rent on a listing is the rent. These are the things that arrive later, and each one should be named before you pay anything:

  • Agent or inspection fee. Arzuz takes none, and no fee should ever be charged to see a room. If you are asked for money to inspect a property, that is a stop sign — see your safety on Arzuz.
  • Caution or security deposit. Common and normal. Ask what has to happen for it to come back, and get the answer before you pay, not after.
  • Water or development levy. A per-session charge on some compounds. Ask whether it is inside the rent or on top of it.
  • Agreement or agency paperwork. Sometimes a landlord's own fee. Ask for the total payable on the day, as one number, in writing.

Ask for the total. "What do I pay on the day I collect the key, all in?" If the answer is a different number from the rent, that is the price of the room — and it is the number you should compare against the next room.

How to compare two rooms in one minute

  1. Convert both rents to per session.
  2. Add every extra — caution, levy, paperwork — to both.
  3. Write down the walking time to the gate you will actually use, at the hour you will actually walk it.
  4. Write down when water comes, and for how long.
  5. Ask who fixes a blown bulb, and how they are reached.
  6. Now compare. The lower rent loses surprisingly often.
